24 Maximum Flow Just as you can model a road map as a directed graph in order to find the shortest path from one point to another, you can also interp ret a directed graph as a <üow network= and use it to answer questions about material üows. Imagine a mate- rial coursing through a system from a source, where the material is produced, to a sink, where it is consumed. The source produces t he material at some steady rate, and the sink consumes the material at the same rate. The <üow= of the mate- rial at any point in the system is intuitively the rate at which the material moves. Flow networks can model many problems, including liquids üowing through pipes, parts through assembly lines, current through electrical networks, and information through communication networks. You can think of each directed edge in a üow network as a conduit for the mate- rial. Each conduit has a stated capacity, given as a maximum rate at which the ma- terial can üow through the conduit, such as 200 gallons of liquid per hour through a pipe or 20 amperes of electrical current through a wire. Verti ces are conduit junctions, and other than the source and sink, material üows through the vertices without collecting in them. In other words, the rate at which material enters a ver- tex must equal the rate at which it leaves the vert ex. We call t his property <üow conservation,= and it is equivalent to Kirchhoff9s current law when the material is electrical current. The goal of the maximum-üow problem is to compute the greatest rate for ship- ping material from the source to the sink without violating any capacity constraints. It is one of the simplest problems concerning üow networks and, as we shall see in this chapter, this problem can be solved by efficient algorithms. Moreover, other network-üow problems are solvable by adapting the basic techniques used in maximum-üow algorithms. This chapter presents two general methods for solving the maximum-üow prob- lem. We call it a <method= rather than an <algorithm= because it encompasses several implementations with differing running times. The Ford-Fulkerson method depends on three important ideas that transcend the method and are relevant to many üow algorithms and problems: residual networks, augmenting paths, and cuts. These ideas are essential to the important max-üow min-cut theorem (The- orem 24.6), which characterizes the value of a maximum üow in terms of cuts of the üow network. We end this section by presenting one specific implementation of the Ford-Fulkerson method and analyzing its running time. The Ford-Fulkerson method iteratively increases the value of the üow. It starts with f (u, v) = 0 for all u, v 2 V , giving an initial üow of value 0. Each iteration increases the üow value in G by finding an <augmenting path= in an associated <residual network= G f . The edges of the augmenting path in G f indicate on which edges in G to update the üow in order to increase the üow value. Although each iteration of the Ford-Fulkerson method increases the value of the üow, we9ll see that the üow on any particular edge of G may increase or decrease. Although it might seem counterintuitive to decrease the üow on an edge, doing so may enable üow to increase on other edges, allowing more üow to travel from the source to the sink. The Ford-Fulkerson method, given in the procedure FORD-FULKERSON - METHOD, repeatedly augments the üow until the residual network has no more augmenting paths. The max-üow min-cut theorem shows that upon termination, this process yields a maximum üow.
